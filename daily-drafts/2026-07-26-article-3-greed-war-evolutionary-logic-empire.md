# Greed, War, and the Evolutionary Logic Behind Every Empire

## Hook

In the winter of 1258, the Mongol army under Hulagu Khan arrived at the gates of Baghdad — then the largest, most intellectually sophisticated city in the world, a centre of mathematics, medicine, astronomy, poetry, and trade that had no equivalent anywhere on the planet. Over three weeks of methodical destruction, they killed between two hundred thousand and one million people, threw the contents of the House of Wisdom library into the Tigris (the water ran black with ink, according to contemporary accounts), and salted enough agricultural infrastructure to collapse the region's food production for a generation.

This is not an isolated event in human history. It is one of the more dramatic instances of a pattern so consistent that it appears in every civilisation the archaeological and historical record has preserved. Expansion, extraction, violence, destruction of rivals. The Roman conquest of Carthage, which ended not with a negotiated peace but with the city being physically razed and its fields salted. The Spanish conquest of the Aztec empire, driven by gold and completed with engineered famine. The British East India Company's systematic extraction of Indian wealth, which transformed the subcontinent from one of the world's wealthiest regions to one of its poorest in the space of two centuries.

Why the same pattern? Why, across cultures that had no contact with each other, across centuries, across languages and geographies, does extraction and violence follow the same structural logic?

The answer is not that human beings are uniquely evil. It is that they are, under certain environmental conditions, executing an ancient biological program with catastrophic efficiency.

## The Hidden Driver

Life History Theory is one of the most powerful and underappreciated frameworks in evolutionary biology. The weekly briefing in this repository (June 29, 2026) summarises its core architecture: organisms — including humans — do not have fixed behavioural programs. Instead, they have conditional strategies that shift along a fast-slow continuum in response to environmental signals.

**Fast life history strategy**: Early reproduction, many offspring with less parental investment per child, higher risk tolerance, shorter time horizons, more aggressive resource acquisition, less cooperation. Selected for in environments of instability, scarcity, and unpredictability — where the future is uncertain and the present is all that can be counted on.

**Slow life history strategy**: Later reproduction, fewer offspring with high parental investment, lower risk tolerance, longer time horizons, more investment in cooperation and social capital. Selected for in environments of stability and abundance — where the future can be planned for.

*(Source: weekly-briefings/2026-06-29-human-nature-evolution-psychology.md — Life History Theory: The Fast-Slow Continuum section)*

The critical insight is that psychosocial stress — not just physical scarcity but the *perception* of scarcity, insecurity, and threat — can shift individuals and populations toward faster life history strategies. You do not need to be objectively starving. You need to be uncertain about whether you will have enough.

This is the evolutionary engine of empire. At the individual level, it is the driver of extractive, short-horizon behaviour. At the collective level, it is the driver of military expansion, resource seizure, and the destruction of competitors who might challenge the resource base. What looks from the outside like moral failure — greed, cruelty, ruthlessness — is, from an evolutionary perspective, an adaptation running in an environment it was not designed for at scale.

## Historical Patterns

The Roman Republic's transformation into the Roman Empire is a textbook illustration of the fast-slow continuum being disrupted at civilisational scale.

The Republic was built on a slow-strategy agricultural class: smallholder citizens who owned land, invested in multi-generational civic institutions, and whose political participation was tied to their stake in a stable future. Roman military expansion — initially defensive or opportunistic — brought back slaves by the hundreds of thousands. Slave-worked latifundia (large estates) undercut smallholder agriculture. Dispossessed farmers moved to Rome, lost their stake in the future, and became the urban poor: a class with fast life history pressures, nothing to conserve, and everything to gain from charismatic strongmen who promised redistribution and glory.

The Republic that fell to Julius Caesar was not destroyed by him. It was destroyed by the economic conditions that activated fast strategies in the population that had previously maintained slow ones. Caesar and Augustus were the product of that pressure, not its cause.

The Mongol conquests followed a similar logic but from the other direction. The Mongolian steppe is one of the most environmentally unstable inhabited ecosystems on the planet — subject to severe winters called *dzud* that can kill ninety percent of a herd in a single season. The populations that survived on the steppe evolved, culturally and biologically, toward fast strategies: mobility over accumulation, raiding over building, dispersal over concentration. What Genghis Khan achieved was the militarisation and organisation of these fast strategies at an unprecedented scale. The destruction the Mongols wrought was not a departure from their adapted behaviour — it was an amplification of it.

The East India Company is the industrial-era example. A private corporation, accountable to shareholders whose time horizon was the next dividend, systematically extracted the productive capacity of a subcontinent while using military force to prevent the development of competing local industries. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 in Bengal deliberately structured land tenure to create a class of local intermediaries (zamindars) with fast-strategy incentives: extract maximum rent from peasants in the current season, with no stake in soil health, agricultural investment, or long-term productivity. The famine that resulted — the Bengal Famine of 1770, which killed perhaps ten million people — was not a natural disaster. It was the predictable outcome of imposing fast-strategy institutional logic on a slow-strategy agricultural system.

The CIA's economic warfare operations of the Cold War period followed the same playbook with added bureaucratic language. Operation CONDOR across Latin America, the overthrow of Mossadegh in Iran in 1953, the destabilisation of Allende in Chile in 1973 — all structured to prevent the development of slow-strategy economic institutions (land reform, nationalisation, long-term industrial development) in favour of fast-strategy extractive arrangements that served short-term US and corporate interests. The mechanism was different; the evolutionary logic was identical.

Marketers and policy makers use Life History Theory without naming it. Advertising research consistently shows that conditions of scarcity or threat (real or manufactured) shift consumer behaviour toward impulsive, short-horizon purchases. Political polling shows that voter behaviour becomes more authoritarian — more fast-strategy — under conditions of economic insecurity. These are not random correlations. They are the adaptive system responding to environmental signals exactly as it was designed to.

## Modern Echoes

Silicon Valley's foundational mythology — "move fast and break things," growth-at-all-costs, the 18-month pivot, the 10x return — is fast life history strategy applied to capital allocation. The venture capital model is structurally fast: short time horizons (fund cycles of 10 years), high-risk tolerance (most investments are expected to fail), and no mechanism for accounting for externalities. The environmental damage, the labour displacement, the social disruption caused by platform monopolies — these are costs that do not appear in the investor's ledger because the investor's time horizon ends before the consequences fully land.

The housing markets in most major cities operate identically. Real estate investment as a financial instrument is an extraction of value from the slow-strategy need for housing security, by actors with fast-strategy time horizons. The people most harmed are those with fast-strategy pressures: insecure tenants who cannot plan beyond their current lease, who cannot invest in the neighbourhood, who are displaced before they can accumulate.

This is the cruelty the evolutionary frame reveals: fast-strategy institutions extract from people experiencing fast-strategy pressures. The most extractive operations target the most vulnerable, not out of special malevolence but because vulnerable populations have the least capacity to resist extraction — exactly the condition under which fast strategies are adaptive.

In communities experiencing acute poverty, the activation of fast life history strategies is observable and well-documented: higher rates of early sexual activity and teen pregnancy, higher risk-taking behaviour, more impulsive decision-making, less investment in education and long-term planning. These patterns are routinely pathologised as moral failures — irresponsibility, short-sightedness, poor values. The evolutionary frame reveals them as adaptive responses to an environment of genuine instability and scarcity. You do not plan for the future if the evidence of your environment is that the future cannot be counted on.

## Implications & Self-Reflection

The Life History Theory lens does not excuse extractive behaviour. It locates it. Understanding that the executive who laid off three thousand people in order to hit a quarterly target is executing a fast-strategy program — not from personal malice but from institutional environmental pressure — does not make the three thousand people less harmed. It does, however, tell you where to intervene: not at the level of individual moral instruction but at the level of the institutional conditions that activate fast strategies.

The environmental triggers are: instability, scarcity, unpredictability, and the absence of a stake in the future. Every institution, policy, and social arrangement that increases these triggers — precarious work, insecure housing, extractive debt, punitive welfare, concentrated power — is selecting for fast-strategy behaviour at scale, and then expressing shock at the results.

The ecological framework of life history theory also applies at the personal level. The times in your own life when you have been most extractive in relationships, most impulsive, most short-horizon in your decisions — were you experiencing security or insecurity? The answer, almost certainly, is insecurity. The uncomfortable question is not whether you are capable of those behaviours under pressure. You are. The question is what conditions you are creating — for yourself and others — that activate or deactivate them.
