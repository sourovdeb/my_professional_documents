# The Uncomfortable Truth About the Serotonin Empire

## Hook

Imagine being told that your grief, your emptiness, your inability to get out of bed in the morning is caused by a measurable deficiency — like iron-deficiency anaemia, but for your brain. You have low serotonin. The cure is a pill that corrects the imbalance. The story is clean, medical, blameless. You are not broken; you are just chemically out of balance. Of course you take the pill.

Between the 1990s and the 2010s, this story — the serotonin hypothesis of depression — was delivered to hundreds of millions of patients worldwide. It appeared in television advertisements, in doctors' offices, in patient leaflets. It became the framework through which an entire generation understood their inner lives.

There was one problem. The research never supported it.

A 2005 audit published in *PLoS Medicine* by Lacasse and Leo compared the claims in SSRI advertising directly against the peer-reviewed literature. Their finding was unambiguous: the low-serotonin claim was not present in the research the advertisements implied it rested on. In 2022, an umbrella review by Moncrieff et al. in *Molecular Psychiatry* — covering seventeen systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and large cohort and genetic studies — examined serotonin metabolite levels, receptor binding, transporter density, tryptophan depletion studies, and SERT genetic variants. The conclusion was equally unambiguous: there is "no convincing evidence" that depression is caused by or even associated with lowered serotonin activity.

This is not a minor methodological dispute. It is a structural collapse of the primary marketing narrative for the most widely prescribed class of psychiatric medication in history.

## The Hidden Driver

Why did we accept it so readily? The answer lies deeper than bad journalism or corporate cynicism — it runs into something fundamental about human cognition.

We are a species that cannot tolerate explanatory voids. In the hunter-gatherer environment that shaped our neurology over two hundred thousand years, ambiguity about causation was dangerous. If people in your village are dying and you do not know why, you invent a reason — spirits, contamination, a neighbouring tribe's curse — because a wrong explanation still permits action, and paralysis kills. The brain evolved to generate causal stories even from incomplete data.

Mental suffering is one of the most profound experiences a human can have, and it resists easy narration. It comes from nowhere visible. It does not bleed. The person experiencing it often cannot explain it. The person witnessing it often cannot witness it at all.

Into this explanatory void, the pharmaceutical industry in the 1980s and 90s dropped a story of extraordinary elegance: *your brain is out of balance, and we make the substance that corrects the imbalance.* The story arrived at the moment SSRIs were being developed, not as a conclusion from research but as a marketing positioning decision. The science was extrapolated backward from the drug's mechanism of action — SSRIs increase serotonin availability, therefore depression must involve insufficient serotonin — a logical step that would fail a first-year pharmacology exam.

The kicker: mainstream psychiatry largely dropped the simple chemical-imbalance framing by the early 2000s. The people who were selling the story knew it was no longer tenable before the patients taking the pills did.

## Historical Patterns

This is not the first time a corporate empire built itself on a scientific-sounding narrative that served its commercial interests first.

The tobacco industry's decades-long campaign to manufacture doubt about the smoking-cancer link is the canonical example: fund friendly researchers, attack hostile findings on methodological grounds, flood the public communication space with ambiguity. The goal was never to win the scientific debate — it was to delay the loss of it long enough to extract maximum revenue.

The pharmaceutical industry's use of the serotonin hypothesis followed the same architecture. Janssen's esketamine (Spravato), marketed as a "breakthrough" for treatment-resistant depression, illustrates the modern iteration. Of three short-term Phase 3 trials, only one — TRANSFORM-2 — hit its primary endpoint. TRANSFORM-1 missed significance entirely. TRANSFORM-3 missed by the narrowest margin (p = 0.059). The FDA's own advisory committee voted 14-2 to approve despite six deaths among trial participants, including three suicides, which the FDA described as "difficult to consider as drug related."

The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which has no financial stake in the drug, reviewed identical trial data and declined to recommend it — citing missing head-to-head comparisons and thin long-term evidence.

*(Source: posts/drafts/2026-07-05-esketamine-breakthrough-or-marketing.md)*

The marketing word "breakthrough" appeared in the product name long before the evidence warranted it. This is empire-building by narrative capture: establish the frame, fund the research that confirms the frame, and by the time regulators catch up, the revenue has been extracted.

Policy makers absorb these narratives too. When the serotonin story became the public understanding of depression, it shifted the centre of gravity of mental health policy toward pharmacological intervention and away from social and structural causes — housing insecurity, childhood trauma, labour conditions, loneliness. It is easier to prescribe a pill than to address a housing crisis. The chemical-imbalance story was, among other things, politically convenient.

## Modern Echoes

The mechanism has not disappeared. It has migrated.

Social media platforms are understood by their own internal researchers to worsen adolescent mental health — the 2021 leaked Facebook documents showed this clearly. The response from the platforms has followed the tobacco playbook: fund friendly research, attack hostile findings on methodological grounds, and maintain public ambiguity long enough to keep users engaged and revenue flowing.

In the wellness industry, the same cognitive architecture operates: give people a simple, blameless explanation for their suffering (cortisol, inflammation, toxins, gut microbiome), sell them the corrective product, and bank the revenue before the evidence catches up. The products change; the structure of the story does not.

We are also seeing a re-run at the individual level. The chemical-imbalance narrative taught millions of people to locate their suffering entirely within their own brain chemistry, making structural and relational causes invisible. People who understood their depression as a chemical deficit were less likely to examine their relationships, their working conditions, or the systems in which they were living. The story that should have prompted inquiry became the reason to stop inquiring.

## Implications & Self-Reflection

None of this means that psychiatric medication is worthless. SSRIs help many people — the mechanism through which they help may simply be more complex and individual-specific than the marketing suggested. The esketamine critics are not arguing that no one benefits from it. The argument is about the gap between the evidence base and the claims made about it.

What the chemical-imbalance story reveals is something more important than the efficacy of any individual drug: it reveals our vulnerability to clean explanations. We want our suffering to have a name and a cure. We want the map to fit the territory. When an industry offers us a map that is simple, blameless, and profitable to it, we should ask who drew it and why.

The deepest uncomfortable truth here is not that pharma companies lied — it is that we wanted to be told what they were telling us. The serotonin story colonised public understanding not because it was forced on us but because it answered something we desperately needed answered.

Sitting with the less comfortable truth — that mental suffering is complex, shaped by biology *and* history *and* relationship *and* economics, and that it often does not have a pill-shaped solution — is one of the harder things a human being can do.
