# The Tribal Brain at War: Primal Instincts That Built and Burned Every Empire

## Hook

In April 2026, *Nature Human Behaviour* published findings from Tinshemet Cave in Israel — evidence that Neanderthals and Homo sapiens in the Levantine mid-Middle Palaeolithic (130,000–80,000 years ago) not only coexisted but shared technology, burial customs, and symbolic use of ochre for decoration. The two species that we assume were competitors were, for tens of thousands of years, collaborators. They built cultural innovations together.

Then they diverged. The Neanderthals are gone.

This is not a story about ancient prehistory. It is a story about the specific social machinery that human beings carry inside their skulls — machinery built for a band of thirty, running now on nation-states of hundreds of millions, on corporate hierarchies of thousands, on social media platforms with billions of users. The machine is not broken. It is working exactly as designed. The results are what we'd rather not think about.

## The Hidden Driver

Robert Trivers, whose evolutionary work the *Persuasion* described as "revolutionary" as recently as March 2026, gave us the framework: *reciprocal altruism*, *kin selection*, and *self-deception* are the three pillars of human social behaviour. We cooperate with genetic relatives (kin selection). We cooperate with non-relatives when there is expectation of reciprocal benefit (reciprocal altruism). And we deceive ourselves about our own motives — systematically, neurologically — because a person who genuinely believes they are acting in good faith is a more convincing cooperator than one who knows they are calculating advantage.

The tribal brain does not experience its own tribalism. It experiences it as *obvious moral clarity*. The in-group is not just preferred — it is *correct*. The out-group is not just different — it is *threatening*, *inferior*, or *inhuman*. This is not rhetoric. It is neurology. Brain-imaging studies consistently show that the medial prefrontal cortex — the region associated with attributing full human experience to others — shows reduced activation when subjects view members of extreme out-groups. The dehumanisation is not a choice. It is the brain running its threat-assessment protocol.

In hunter-gatherer bands, this made sense. Your band of thirty was the only people who would share food with you, carry you when injured, nurse you when sick. People outside the band were, in the ancestral environment, statistically more likely to compete for the same resources. The machinery that drove fierce in-group loyalty and deep suspicion of out-groups was selected for millions of years, because the bands that ran it most efficiently survived, and the ones that extended unconditional cooperation to strangers often did not.

We carry that hardware. We have built civilisations on top of it. But we have not replaced it.

## Historical Patterns

The Mongol conquests of the 13th century are perhaps the cleanest historical illustration of the tribal brain at civilisational scale. Genghis Khan's achievement was not military genius alone — it was the engineering of tribal loyalty at scale. He broke the traditional clan-based structure of Mongolian society (where loyalty ran to bloodline) and rebuilt it around *meritocratic unit loyalty* — loyalty to the *arban* (unit of ten), the *zuun* (hundred), the *mingan* (thousand). He made the unit the tribe.

The result was an army of terrifying cohesion and a pattern of treatment that was precisely structured around the in-group/out-group binary. Cities that submitted without resistance were incorporated and often treated generously. Cities that resisted were annihilated — not from cruelty as such, but from a strategic understanding that annihilation communicated, to the next city, the cost of out-group resistance. The pattern repeated, from Samarkand to Baghdad to the Rus principalities, with a regularity that was not emotional but algorithmic.

The Roman expansion followed a structurally identical logic. Rome was not, initially, a conquering power by ideological design. It was a city-state that faced repeated threats from neighbouring tribes and cities, and resolved each threat by absorption: defeated enemies were often granted Roman citizenship over time, their elites integrated into Roman institutions. The in-group expanded — but the boundary between in-group and out-group was maintained with absolute clarity. At the frontier, "barbarian" was not merely a descriptor. It was a moral category.

What destroyed Rome was not an external enemy but the internal collapse of the tribal boundary. By the fourth century, Germanic soldiers filled the legions, Germanic commanders led Roman armies, and Germanic leaders held Roman titles. The out-group had not been absorbed — the category had dissolved. When Alaric sacked Rome in 410 CE, he was a *foederatus* — an ally under Roman treaty — who had not been paid. The tribal brain, deprived of its clear in-group/out-group map, had stopped functioning as a cohesion mechanism.

The colonial empires of the 19th and 20th centuries demonstrate the same mechanism with the added variable of racial categorisation — a technology for maintaining in-group/out-group boundaries at the scale of continents. The Belgian Congo's rubber atrocities, in which an estimated 10 million Congolese died between 1885 and 1908, were not aberrations of an otherwise humane system. They were the tribal brain's logic applied to industrial extraction: the colonised population was, by the logic of the system, the out-group — different in kind, existing to serve the in-group's material needs, with no claim to reciprocal consideration.

**How policy-makers and marketers exploit this.** Modern nation-states do not educate their populations out of the tribal brain. They weaponise it. Immigration policy in every Western democracy is structured around the activation of in-group threat responses: the out-group (immigrants, refugees, asylum seekers) is presented as competing for resources that belong to the in-group. The rhetoric is almost universally about *replacement* — not economic data, not empirical studies of labour market impact, but the primal signal: *they* are taking something that is *ours*.

Political advertising research (including studies by the Wesleyan Media Project) consistently shows that negative ads outperform positive ones, and that ads activating threat responses outperform ads activating aspiration — because the threat circuit is older, faster, and more powerful than the rational-aspiration circuit. Fear is not a manipulation of human psychology. It is an accurate read of it.

The CIA's playbook applies identical logic. Cold War-era regime change operations (Iran 1953, Guatemala 1954, Chile 1973) were not presented internally as imperial resource acquisition. They were presented as defence of the in-group against Communist contamination. The tribal brain makes it genuinely difficult for operators to recognise that they are the aggressor, because the threat is real *from inside the tribal frame*. Self-deception, as Trivers noted, is not a bug — it is a feature that makes the in-group warrior more effective.

## Modern Echoes

The tribal brain does not require nation-states or armies. It runs perfectly well on comment sections.

Platform research (Facebook's own 2021 leaked documents, the MIT Media Lab's viral content studies) shows that content activating tribal outrage travels on average 70% faster than neutral content and reaches six times as many people. The platforms did not design this — they discovered it and then optimised for it, because engagement is what the business model rewards and outrage is the highest-engagement emotional state. The tribal brain, running at 3 billion-user scale, generates the same in-group/out-group sorting, the same dehumanisation of the other side, the same absolute moral clarity — in the comment section of a cooking video, given the right context.

The modern workplace runs its own tribal dynamics. Any organisation large enough to have departments will have developed inter-departmental tribalism: the Marketing team and the Product team are the *Hatfields and McCoys*, unable to accurately attribute good faith to the other side. Corporate mergers fail at rates above 50% partly because the acquiring company's tribal brain classifies the acquired company as an out-group to be assimilated rather than cooperated with — and the acquired company's tribal brain prepares for exactly that.

## Implications & Self-Reflection

The tribal brain is not an evil to be extirpated. It is the engine of the loyalty, solidarity, and fierce protectiveness that make human beings extraordinary social animals. A parent defending their child, a community rallying around someone in crisis, a workforce that actually functions as a team — all of these run on the same machinery.

The question is not whether to have a tribal brain. It is whether you can see yours in operation.

The moment you find yourself experiencing an out-group as uniformly malevolent — a political party, a nationality, a professional category, a user base, a religion — you are not seeing clearly. You are running the threat-assessment protocol, and the protocol does not distinguish between a genuine predator and a frightened stranger who reminds the system of one.

The archaeological record from Tinshemet Cave suggests that the same brain that built every empire and fought every war was also capable of 50,000 years of cross-species cultural collaboration. That capacity did not disappear. It is available to you, right now, in every interaction you choose to see as an encounter with a full human being rather than a representative of an out-group.

That choice is not soft. It is the hardest thing the tribal brain ever has to do.
